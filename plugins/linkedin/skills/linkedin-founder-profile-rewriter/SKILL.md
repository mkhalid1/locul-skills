# LinkedIn founder profile rewriter

## The claim this skill is built on

Almost every founder profile is a resume with the job titles updated. Chronology, employer names, duties, a headline reading "Founder and CEO at Something Ltd". That document has a real audience and serves it well: recruiters, who scan for role, tenure and employer in that order. It is the wrong document for a founder who wants inbound from customers, partners or investors, and it is wrong in a way no amount of better writing fixes, because the problem is not the prose.

The obvious approach fails because it treats the profile as one document that gets different adjectives depending on who you want: warmer language for customers, harder numbers for investors. That is not what changes. What changes is the ordering principle, the unit of proof, the primary call to action, what belongs in Featured, and which words go in the fields at all. A profile aimed at customers opens on the problem you solve and proves it with an artefact a buyer can open. A profile aimed at investors opens on the market and proves it with traction. Those are not two tones. They are two documents that happen to live at the same URL.

So the first step is choosing, not writing. That choice has downstream consequences the platform enforces, several of which cannot be undone.

## Step 0: the audience decision, which is the whole method

Name one primary audience before touching a field. Four branches.

**Recruiter.** They scan for role, tenure and employer. Chronological Experience carries the document, the headline states role and domain, skills matter because recruiters filter on them, and the primary action is Connect. If this is genuinely your goal, most standard profile advice is already correct for you.

**Customer.** They arrive from a post, a comment, a search or a message, answering one question: does this person understand my problem well enough to be worth a reply. The ordering principle is problem first, evidence second, company third. Proof is an artefact they can open in under ten seconds: a teardown, a template, a case write-up, a working demo. The current Experience entry describes who you serve and what changes for them, not your responsibilities. The custom button points at the thing that answers their question, not at a booking form they are not ready for.

**Partner.** They are checking whether an integration, a referral or a co-marketing deal is worth a meeting. They want scope, adjacency and reliability: who you already work with, what you plug into, what you do not do. Proof is a named category of existing relationship, and About has to make the boundary of your product obvious, because a partner's first fear is that you compete with them.

**Investor.** They are checking whether the market is big, whether you are credible in it, and whether anyone is using the thing. The ordering principle is market, then traction, then you. Proof is numbers rather than opinions, and Featured carries something that survives being forwarded to a colleague without you in the room.

**The two-audience branch, which is where most profiles fail.** If you honestly serve two, do not split the fields. Pick the primary and give the secondary exactly one surface: one Featured item, or one line inside the current Experience entry, never both and never the headline. The reason is mechanical. A headline speaking to customers and investors at once contains a clause for each, so the first clause is no longer the thing a customer needs to read, and the headline is truncated in more places than any other field: search results, feed bylines, comment bylines and the mobile app all cut it differently. A hedged headline is not a compromise, it is a headline whose visible half is now hedge.

**The cannot-tell branch.** If you cannot name a primary audience, do not write the profile yet. Count the last twenty inbound conversations you were pleased to receive, by type. A clear winner is your primary. With no meaningful inbound history at all, pick the audience whose next ninety days pays a bill, write for that, mark the profile provisional and revisit in a quarter. A provisional profile aimed at one audience beats a permanent one aimed at everybody.

## Why the order matters

The rewrite order is: audience, headline, About opening, Featured plan, current Experience entry, Skills, cover image, primary action and custom button, activity check.

Each step consumes the decision above it. Skills chosen before the audience decision get chosen from your CV, so you list what you are good at rather than what your audience filters on. Featured items chosen before the About promise get chosen from your favourites rather than as evidence for a specific claim. And writing About last, which most people do because it feels like a summary, produces a summary of the profile instead of the argument the profile exists to make.

One out-of-order move is expensive rather than merely wasteful. Building Featured before the audience decision is the worst of them, because removing an item from Featured is irreversible according to LinkedIn's own documentation, checked August 2026, so you cannot iterate by undo.

## Step 1: the headline

Write the audience word and the proof word into the first clause. Everything after the first clause should be treated as text you may lose.

**LinkedIn documents no headline character limit.** Its Help Centre page on editing the headline gives steps and no number, and the Profile Edit API lists the field with no maximum length, checked August 2026. Third parties consistently report 220 and may well be right, but it is not a LinkedIn figure, and the older figure of 120 is what much surviving advice was written against. So the method does not depend on a count: front-load, then paste the draft into the editor and see where it stops accepting characters on the day you write. That is the only reliable number.

On search, say only what is supportable. LinkedIn documents that profile details such as location, workplace and skills feed content ranking, but that page is about **feed relevance, not people search**, checked August 2026. It publishes nothing on search ranking or field weighting. The repeated claim that the headline outranks About has no primary source; the pages cited for it are member-written collaborative articles hosted on linkedin.com, not LinkedIn documentation. Profile text is searchable, so use the words your audience types. Anything more precise is folklore, and a profile built on an unverifiable weighting theory reads like a listing.

## Step 2: the About section

The About section moved to the **top of the profile in March 2024**, documented by LinkedIn, so it now sits above Experience. Any advice assuming a reader reaches Experience first predates that change.

The count at which About truncates behind "see more" is contested: published figures include 200, 220, 265 to 275 and 300, split inconsistently between desktop and mobile, with no agreed methodology. Do not design to a number. Write the first two to three lines as a self-contained unit that works if nothing below is ever opened, then check it on both a desktop browser and the mobile app, because they differ.

The commonly cited 2,600 character limit is third-party and LinkedIn's About page carries no number. **The only length guidance LinkedIn publishes is qualitative:** "Ideally, you should limit the text to one or two paragraphs", checked August 2026. It is the single LinkedIn-attributable statement on About length that exists, and it points the same way the truncation problem does.

## Step 3: Featured, which is the conversion surface

This is where a visitor stops reading and starts doing something, and it has documented mechanics that change how you use it, all checked August 2026.

It accepts **exactly four kinds of thing**: LinkedIn posts you created or reshared, articles you authored and published on LinkedIn, links to external sites, and uploaded media such as images, documents, presentations and videos. You **cannot** feature posts from Events you are attending or Groups you belong to.

You **must have already published** a post or article before you can feature it. This is the single most common blocker in a rewrite plan: three ideal Featured items are chosen, two of them do not exist yet, and the profile cannot go live in its intended state until they are written and published. Sequence it deliberately, publish first and feature second.

**Removal is irreversible.** Reordering needs at least two items. The section is **hidden entirely when it has no content**, so an empty Featured is not a neutral gap in the page, it is the absence of the whole surface. Items are **unlimited but only 400 are displayed**, which in practice means order carries far more weight than count and the first three items do the work.

Uploaded media limits: 100 MB, 300 pages, one million words, and 120 megapixels of image resolution, which is genuinely different from the 36 megapixel cap on feed post media. Supported formats are PDF, PPT and PPTX, DOC and DOCX, JPG and JPEG, PNG, and GIF with the first frame only. **Documents cannot be uploaded from mobile**, so plan the Featured build as a desktop job, on Windows or Mac. Premium Business, Sales Navigator and Recruiter subscribers can also pin content from other profile sections into Featured.

Featured item image dimensions are not documented by LinkedIn and third-party figures disagree badly, ranging from 502 x 262 to 1200 x 644. Do not design to any of them. Upload, look at the preview, adjust.

## Step 4: the current Experience entry

The current role entry does almost all the work and the older ones are context. Rewrite it around who you serve and what changes for them, in the branch you chose.

The reported limits of 100 characters for the job title and 2,000 for the description come from aggregator sites with no primary source and no stated methodology. Treat both as approximate and check in the editor rather than writing to them.

One documented detail that most rewrites never touch: **skills attached to roles in your Experience section do not count against the Skills section cap.** LinkedIn states this plainly. That makes the Experience entry unmetered space for the vocabulary your audience uses, and it is free.

## Step 5: Skills

**A profile can list up to 100 skills**, documented by LinkedIn and checked August 2026. The limit used to be 50, and telling a reader to "use all 50" is now wrong. The 100 cap applies **only to the Skills section**, not to skills attached to Experience entries.

Order matters more than volume, because a visitor sees the top of the list without expanding it and most never expand. Put the branch-relevant skills first: the words a customer uses for their problem, or the words a partner uses for the category you sit in. Do not fill 100 slots because the ceiling exists. Two commonly repeated constraints appear in no LinkedIn document at all, an endorsement cap and a limit of five skills per experience entry, so plan around neither and test in the editor if it matters.

## Step 6: the cover image

Recommended dimensions are **1584 x 396 px**, documented. The maximum file size for profile and background images is **8 MB**, documented, and the 4 MB figure repeated across many blogs is wrong. **File type must be PNG or JPG; GIFs are not supported** for profile or background photos. The profile photo itself must be between 400 x 400 and 7680 x 4320 px. LinkedIn now calls this the **cover image** rather than the background photo, and Premium tiers can make it a slideshow. All checked August 2026.

The 4:1 aspect ratio rule that circulates alongside 1584 x 396 is not a LinkedIn constraint. LinkedIn publishes a recommended pixel dimension and nothing about ratios, and how much is visible changes with window size and screen resolution. Keep anything load-bearing well inside the frame, away from the edges and clear of the region the profile photo covers, then check it in a narrow browser window and in the mobile app.

**A personal profile cover is not a company Page cover.** A Page cover is 1512 x 256 px, the Life tab main image 1128 x 376, a Page logo 400 x 400 recommended, all PNG or JPG with a **3 MB** maximum. Different asset, different numbers, and they get mixed up constantly. Asking a designer for "the LinkedIn banner size" without saying which is how a profile ends up wearing a stretched Page asset.

## Step 7: the primary action, and the Creator Mode correction

**Creator Mode no longer exists and you must never instruct anyone to turn it on.** LinkedIn removed the on/off toggle in **March 2024**, stated in LinkedIn's own change table. Profile hashtags, the "talks about" topics in the introduction, were removed **beginning February 2024**. In the same period the About section moved to the top of the profile, and members gained the option to choose **Follow or Connect** as the profile's primary action, with Follow set as the default for accounts that had had Creator Mode switched on.

Nothing replaced it. The features it bundled were opened to everyone: LinkedIn Live, Audio Events, Newsletters, which still carry their own access criteria, creator analytics, the Analytics and tools hub, the Follow link, and eligibility to be surfaced as a suggested creator. All documented, checked August 2026.

The modern equivalent is two settings. **Set the primary profile action** to Follow or Connect, and **add a custom button** to the introduction. Branch rule: choose Follow when you publish regularly and the audience is customer, partner or investor, because it lets people subscribe without spending an invitation and without you accepting anything. Choose Connect for the recruiter branch, or when you do not publish and a follow buys the visitor nothing. Point the custom button at whatever answers the visitor's immediate question in your branch, not at a calendar link they are not ready for.

Worth checking on your own profile: any account with Creator Mode on when the toggle went was defaulted to Follow, so founders who never revisited it are often showing the wrong action for the audience they now want.

## Step 8: the activity check, and the 45-day window

The Activity section sits directly below Featured and **shows all activity for the past 45 days**, documented. That window has a real consequence for a rewrite.

A profile rewritten to attract customers, sitting above 45 days of comments on hiring posts and congratulations on other people's job moves, contradicts itself within one scroll. You cannot shorten the window and there is nothing to switch off, so you can only outrun it, which takes about six weeks.

Run the check before you ship. If the last 45 days hold fewer than a handful of items pointing the same way as the new profile, you have two honest options: start publishing and commenting in the new direction on the day the rewrite goes live and treat it as provisional for six weeks, or move any deadline that depends on the profile reading as coherent. If a launch is three weeks away, the activity window is the binding constraint, not the copy.

## Worked example

A founder of an invented scheduling tool for independent physiotherapy clinics. Current profile: headline "Founder and CEO at an invented scheduling company", About section a career history opening with where she studied, Featured empty, 31 skills led by "Microsoft Excel", cover image a stretched Page banner, primary action Connect, and 45 days of activity that is mostly comments on posts about hiring engineers. Goal: more clinic owners booking demos. Also raising a small round in about nine months.

**Audience decision.** Demos pay bills this quarter and the raise is nine months out, so primary is customer, secondary is investor with exactly one surface.

**Headline and About.** The first clause of the headline carries the audience word and the problem word, so a clinic owner scanning a comment byline knows within four words that this is about their diary, and the company name leaves that clause. The first two lines of About state the problem in the clinic owner's own words. Both are pasted into the editor and trimmed to what the field actually accepts and where it actually collapses, on desktop and on the phone, rather than to a number from a blog.

**Featured.** Three slots planned: a no-signup demo link, a write-up of how a clinic recovered lost slots, and the secondary surface, a post about the category the investor audience cares about. Two do not exist yet, which is the blocking item. Nothing is deleted from Featured during planning, since removal cannot be undone.

**Everything else.** The current Experience entry is rewritten around who she serves and what changes, and its attached skills carry the clinic vocabulary at no cost against the cap. Skills are reordered so clinic words lead. New cover at 1584 x 396, PNG, under 8 MB, text clear of the edges and of the area the profile photo covers; the Page banner is discarded as the wrong asset. Primary action set to Follow, custom button on the demo link.

**Verdict.** Ship the customer profile. Give the investor audience one Featured slot and one sentence in the current Experience entry, and leave the raise-facing rewrite until the round is open. The blocking item is not copy: two of three Featured slots point at posts that do not exist, so the profile cannot reach its final state until those are published. Add 45 days of hiring-shaped activity sitting under the About section and the earliest date this reads as coherent to a clinic owner is roughly six weeks after she starts publishing in the new direction. That date drives the launch plan, not the day the copy is finished.

## Failure modes

**The hedged profile.** Two audiences addressed at once in every field. It reads as reasonable, offends nobody and converts nobody. From outside it looks like a headline with a comma and two clauses, an About section describing a career rather than a problem, and Featured items each aimed at a different person.

**The retired instruction.** The rewrite says to turn on Creator Mode or add "talks about" topics. The toggle went in March 2024, the hashtags from February 2024. The symptom is a founder hunting through settings for a switch that is not there, then assuming they lack some tier.

**The 50-skill ceiling.** The plan stops at 50 because the advice said so, or worse tells the founder to "use all 50" as though the ceiling were a target. The cap is 100, and it applies only to the Skills section.

**The invisible Featured section.** Featured is left empty because nothing seemed good enough. The section is hidden entirely when empty, so the visitor does not see a gap, they see a profile with no conversion surface, and nothing signals anything is missing.

**The irreversible tidy-up.** Someone clears out old Featured items intending to re-add the good ones later. Removal cannot be undone, and if the item was a post that has since been deleted or an external link that has moved, it is gone.

**The unpublished feature.** The plan names three Featured items and two of them are posts nobody has written. Nothing errors, the profile simply sits in a half-finished state for weeks while everyone assumes the rewrite shipped.

**The wrong banner.** A Page cover at 1512 x 256, a file squeezed to a 4 MB ceiling that never applied, or a GIF that will not upload at all. The symptom is a cover that looks stretched, crops its own text at some window widths, or refuses to save.

**The 45-day contradiction.** The profile says one thing and the activity immediately beneath it says another. It is invisible to the person who wrote the profile, because they look at the fields they edited and not at the feed under them.

## What this skill does not do

- It cannot see your profile, your visitor analytics or your inbound. It works entirely from what you tell it about who you want, so a wrong answer there produces a coherent profile aimed at the wrong people.
- It does not publish the character counts for the headline, About, or experience fields, because LinkedIn documents none of them and the circulating figures disagree. Count in the editor if you need a number.
- It does not write posts, comments, document posts or direct messages, and the Featured plan usually depends on posts that do not exist yet.
- It cannot verify that any figure here is still current on the day you read it. Everything above was checked in August 2026 and LinkedIn changes profile mechanics without announcement, as the 2024 changes demonstrate.
- It cannot tell you whether the audience you named is the right one commercially. That is a business question, and a message-testing panel or a set of customer calls answers it better than any rewrite.
